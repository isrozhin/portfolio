Mimo: Balance sheet reconciliation

Designed and delivered a balance sheet reconciliation workflow which helps accountants save hours of detective work by analyzing transactions, highlighting anomalies and suggesting actions to prepare their books for month-end close.

About the project

Every business needs to reconcile their balance sheet to ensure correct reporting. Accountants need to know all the details about each client and do a lot of investigation and sense-checking to make sure the numbers are right. Given that every client is different, this job takes a lot of effort and requires several different tools. We at Mimo took a stab at simplifying this process by outsourcing the detective work to the smart workflow which does all the preparation in minutes and lets accountant review prepared results rather than doing all the work manually.

In parallel with developing a new design framework

The timing of this project overlapped with us developing new Mimo design framework. It was an enabler without which balance sheet reconciliation would've been less flexible and more linked to a concrete actions. Given that all accounting firms are different, and all their clients are different, we decided that it's not worth to design a product with hard constraints and agreed to use a new design framework to enable agentic design. As a bonus we got an opportunity to stress test it together with a product.

Discovery, discovery, discovery

First, we ran a couple of workshops to map out initial user flows and list key hypotheses for the balance sheet reconciliation. Since none of us worked as an accountant, we needed to paint a picture from scratch.

Next, we started to prepare for user testing. Initially we followed the traditional way and made quick Figma prototypes. After first round of internal discussions and testing with our partners we decided to try another approach and made a fully functional realistic claude prototype. 2 reasons behind that:

  1. The design framework was genuinely new, and some details and interactions needed careful testing
  2. It's hard to do a thorough tests with static data and mockups. Highly detailed realistic prototype decreases the risk of misunderstanding and lifts more concrete findings.

We conducted 6 testing sessions total. Some hypotheses were proven, some things needed to be rethought, but in the end we were happy with the solution.

Development in parallel

Devs started implementation before we finished all the testing. This approach helped us speed things up: by the time we were done with the solutions, developers were ready to do the final polishing.

Launch and first results

We opened a beta for our partners about 2 months after we started. First results were controversial: some things worked fine, some things needed tweaking. The solution itself was fine: nobody got confused in terms of where to go and what to click. The problem was in quality of the conversation and results reliability: it wasn't consistently good and sometimes went off-track. We took these learnings to rethink some parts of our product.

Iterations

We didn't take the beta down, but continued iterating on it. The pace was quite fast with the goal to deliver continuous fixes in 2 weeks. We also made some changes to the solution, and decided to start with the most demanded reconciliations instead of doing everything at once.

Done, but still ongoing

We continue working on the balance sheet reconciliation, focusing on batches of prioritized recs at a time.

Mimo design framework: from SaaS to agentic in 1 month

Given Mimo's ambition to deliver the smartest solution for accountants, our design team needed to transform the way we work and deliver solutions. Our goal was to shift things in a way that would allow us to focus on discovery and problem solving rather than targeted custom solutions. Here's how we did it.

From unnecessary complexity to meaningful simplicity

Accounting is complex enough on its own. Accountants have dozens of tools which they use alongside their excel sheets. They have hundreds of clients and spend a tremendous amount of time on repetitive tasks rather than on delivering their expertise. Initially Mimo was aiming at solving that, but in a rather old fashion way. Specific interfaces targeting specific problems, narrow use cases, limited capabilities – it was all fine until the tools like Claude started showing that it could be done differently.

This was the moment we decided to pivot the way our product works, and give accountants flexible tools that will work under the hood and give them full control of the results.

For the design team it meant that we needed to revisit the way we do things.

Design system upgrade

Not just components, but the system as a whole. The one we had was quite detailed and well-built given the limited prioritisation it got, but it didn't fit the new reality of the tool where the solution isn't set in stone, but adapts to user's request.

Enrich the design system

It often gets mistreated and slimmed down to a component library, but we believed it should cover the whole experience, from how the product sounds to how it feels to the user.

Adjust the design process

Changing from designing SaaS to agentic product is quite a big leap, and it meets a lot of friction internally. We needed to do a lot of discovery and iterate on the process until we got to a place that felt right.

How did we make it happen?

Discovery

Probably the hardest part of the whole shift-to-agentic story. Partially it's easy – check how other agentic solutions work, understand what's the experience like, see what can be adopted, what should be skipped, etc. The hardest part is to shift the mindset from “this card should contain this information” to “I don't know exactly what will user interact with, but here're all scenarios we need to consider”. In the end of the day, we landed on the most practical concept we could find at a time – generative ui adjusted to our product needs.

Constant sync with product and devs.

We worked very closely together and shared every bit of what we found out to make sure the design framework will be easy to implement, easy to follow and, most importantly, easy to reuse and evolve.

Iterations

We tried dozens of different ways of how things could look, feel and behave. Given limited time we were doing this as a prep-work for a new product feature, so the use cases were scoped to support them first, with ambition to grow and evolve the framework later.

Polishing and stress-testing

After iterating we landed on the framework that was aligned with our plans most. Given that the new product capability was already being implemented, we got a chance to stress-test the framework and the product at the same time.

A couple of flaws were found and fixed, without major time sink.

New Mimo agentic framework

In only a month we managed to come up with a new design framework, covering all must haves for the planned H2 releases. From conversation to how user can adjust what they see – the majority of things was covered.

It allowed us to focus on discovery for the major product updates and save time on designing solutions – framework already covered that. Is it ideal? I'd say not really, it has its flaws. But at this point it allows Mimo to move on much faster than before, which is the main goal for now.

Anyfin: holistic experience

Bringing holistic user experience into the app

I joined Anyfin in April 2022. Shortly after that, recession hit the EU. In this new reality our service became even more relevant for people with debts. Therefore we needed to focus even more on delivering the best experience to our existing and potential users.

Status quo

Originally, Anyfin was focusing on personal finances overall. We had savings, financial insights, etc. etc.

But since the situation has changed, and the economy didn't look as good as it used to, we decided that we want to focus on the thing we do best – helping people in debt.

So, amongst other things, we needed to adjust our product and make it fit our new focus area.

Main problems to solve

Four things were on top of our mind:

  • Our product didn't have a clear focus
  • We had a “pick and choose” concept, which made the product not intuitive enough
  • The product was not flexible enough. For instance, if we wanted to add a new feature, it was really hard to find a proper place in the app for it.
  • Since things were scattered here and there, app lacked consistency

To make our product fit the new strategy, these issues needed to be solved, as quickly as possible. And not just “ad-hoc” solved, but properly fixed, so that the new structure could be future-proof and support the next improvements and experiments.

Research

First things first. To deliver the best result for our users, we needed to know them even better than we did at that moment. Of course we had some previous research results, but they were from the “calm times”, therefore they didn't really work in this case.

We knew that if we want to get the best result, we need to understand not only users behaviour, but also their background. How did they get into debt, what were the triggers, how do they deal with it on a daily basis, how do they feel about it, and other essential aspects one needs to know to design a good experience.

Long story short, we conducted 20 user interviews, each 1,5 hours long. Users were divided in 2 groups, and each group was also split in half – our current users vs non-users. Doing this allowed us to get the bigger picture and understand their real expectations. To sum up the outcome, I can say that we got:

  • Key scenarios for each user group
  • Customer journey from the debt-free life to dealing with debts using Anyfin service
  • Users' expectations, both emotional and informational
  • 20+ insights which we actually were not aware of
  • 10+ proofed hypotheses

Design

With the research results in place, we started the design process.

We decided to put a very simple idea in the core – to make our service work based on how people in debt see their finances. And it is as simple as ”the money I have vs the money I have to spend”.

With this idea set, we started defining the purpose of each part of our app.

Even though our product might feel simple, it's a complicated financial service with tons of different scenarios. And all of those needed to stay, but to be transformed into intuitive flows.

So we did another pre-work – mapped out every key scenario and put it in the chart, to keep things high-level and easy-to-discuss with everyone.

When all the flows were done, it was time to move forward and make the final designs. We've started with the ideal version, and then scaled it down to feasible steps that could be quickly released.

Home and the main insights

Debt management

Problem → Fix

The launch of the update went quite smoothly. We kept an eye on all the key metrics to be sure that business is not affected by the implemented changes.

And, as always, shortly after release, we noticed one improvement we should make. Customers were a bit confused by the key insights we put on top of the home tab. There were both technical and copy issues.

The copy problem we fixed very quickly, so this confusion has been removed. Technical issues, however, needed more time to be investigated and solved.

We had a choice: give it as much time as needed or act quickly and maybe find something more valuable for the users. We chose the second option.

After a quick round of discussions, brainstorms and some ideation we found a good solution, which made sense for both users and our business. We decided to give the top estate for our core refinancing product, since at that point of time it was the most valuable thing for the user. And also the product we're proud of.

We implemented this change quickly and kept monitoring all key metrics. It turned out to be a successful bet – reach-outs to the Customer support decreased, customers stopped missing their payments and the key info about their refinanced loans.

This update also allowed us to do a better communication about customer's payments. We already had this improvement planned, but, thanks to this update, it happened much faster than it could.

Outcome 🔮

In only 3 months we managed to release the biggest update for Anyfin app, which is only the beginning of our journey. We made a flexible structure that fits our short and long term goals. We made a base for future improvements of the product. We made it scalable. It became more intuitive. End of chapter 2.0. Moving on to 2.1.

Intensive care monitoring system

A digital tool for intensive care units

Hospitals are often facing budget limitations. Because of this, expensive equipment could become an issue, which could result in both endangering patients and making hospital staff's work routines unnecessary complicated. Therefore, our client came up with an idea of an accessible monitoring system, an additional tool that could be used by both doctors and nurses.

This system was not supposed to completely replace professional medical monitors in the near future. Its main purpose was to simplify the doctors' and nurses' daily life and automate those processes that could and should be automated.

Difficulties

Right after the first brief we identified several points that were very important to keep in mind at every step of this project.

  • Medicine is a huge area of expertise – but we must thoroughly understand at least a part of it that is related to the intense care
  • Doctors and nurses can be less familiar with digital environment – so we have to be extra careful and use as many medical industry familiar patterns as we can
  • Doctors will have very little time for us – so we must not distract them from important matters and prepare questions very carefully
  • We have to triple check every solution – because the cost of error can be catastrophically high

Research

First, we conducted an online interview with some people who work at the hospital.

We wanted to understand:

  • how a typical doctor's working day looks
  • the responsibilities for different roles
  • how they act in a critical situation
  • the daily work pain points

We needed all this information to:

  • map out a typical doctor's or nurse's daily scenario
  • find situations in which the future system could be useful
  • understand when the system should fade into the background and not interfere with real work

After we drafted the scenario, we went to the hospital to observe the actual doctor's and nurse's work.

Our goals were to:

  • check if the scenario was correct
  • evaluate potential features
  • dive deeper into the context of each situation

In the end, we managed not only to observe the intensive care unit's work, but also conducted several more interviews with the nurses and the head of the unit. This got us several insights and a wish list for the system from those who will actually use it on a daily basis.

Step back and two steps forward

When the research was done, we've started sketching. But we quickly realised that the drafts we made were too UI focused, which made it too difficult to evaluate them. The final solution should be 100% based on the scenarios we got from the research. So, we decided to play it safe and took a step back to do another round of analysis.

We divided the general scenario into 21 micro-scenarios – specific situations that occur every day in the intensive care unit.

After that, we broke them down to the specific actions that the doctor does in each specific situation. In addition to that we also complemented each scenario with its alternative version, to make sure we have a solution for the case when things don't go as they should.

In this exercise we didn't do any visual designs, just the logic part of the future system. This allowed us to concentrate on the real life context and identify so-called “Attention focuses” – what exactly is on one's mind at a certain point of the scenario.

This approach is very useful when you need to find a solution to real user problems. Also, after this exercise is done, designs are very easy to check and test.

Design

Initially, the client had already had the MVP version of the product. But it was a bit difficult to understand, and it needed too much attention to itself. To make it work you needed to configure everything manually.

We agreed to change the whole thing and make it as simple and intuitive as possible, so that users don't have to spend a lot of time studying it.

We decided to start from the general patient monitoring and a patient card. These functions mostly duplicate professional medical bedside monitors, so we started looking for references and common patterns.

General monitoring

Selected patient monitoring

Some solutions may seem a bit old-fashioned, but they were chosen on purpose. They make it easier for the doctor to work with the system.

Patient prescription list

In the client's version, almost any action with the patient could be logged. We left only the most important things – injections, infusions and food. These are necessary for monitoring the liquid balance in the patient's body, which is a very important indicator.

We also added one more feature - checking prescriptions for all patients in the unit. This greatly simplifies the work of nurses.

Paperwork

The third thing we needed to improve was the paperwork automation. The system can automatically generate all the necessary documents for consumables, tests, reports, and it can store files (images, electrocardiogram recordings, etc.). This saves a lot of time when it comes to preparing daily reports, maintaining medical records and transferring patients between units.

Outcome 🪄

We designed a system that simplifies the daily work for doctors, nurses and heads of departments in intensive care. This required a very deep dive into an incredibly complex sphere. Through careful research and testing, we made an intuitive product. This is especially important for medicine, because the cost of an error can be very high.

Our hypothesis was that the system should retain the traditional equipment patterns, and it should be easier for doctors and nurses to solve their problems in a familiar environment.

Each iteration was tested, and in the end we came to a solution that future users understood without a hint.

Anyfin's Credit score tracker

A playful way to help people understand their debt

Anyfin's main goal at the time was helping people understand and deal with their debts. The “deal” part was already covered with the loan refinance, company's core product. But the “understanding” was a huge field to discover. So we decided that we want to give our users probably the most essential tool to understand their debt situation: a Credit score tracker.

Why Credit score tracker?

It was the first thing we all asked ourselves. At first glance it might seem not very interesting or groundbreaking.

So, naturally, we started by looking at the competition. And, to our surprise, we haven't found one we loved. They all lacked something – sometimes simplicity, sometimes information...

But more importantly such a tool was perfectly aligned with our mission – help people understand and deal with their debt.

With this in mind and the estimated user potential (which we estimated to be quite good) we started working on our Credit score tracker.

But...how?

The idea of a credit score itself is quite simple: show people their score, break it down in a simple way, and that's it.

The tricky part was that there were a bunch of other trackers on the market already.

So the first thing we did was exploring potential solutions. And we had quite a few options in the end.

After a couple of iterations and tons of options we landed on one simple thought: if we want users to use and like our credit score, it should stand out. We took it as a core principle and started over.

Make it stand out

This simple idea turned our project into a very fun one. We focused on delight and simplicity as the key factors that should balance the seriousness of the information we give our users.

Anyfin mascot

First, we brought back Anyfin's old mascot – the dolphin. It was quite well known in Sweden, but Anyfin stopped using it some time ago. So we thought it was a good time for a comeback.

Focus on details

We played with this a bit more and added a diving animation and other sea-related metaphors to illustrate the idea of diving into the unknown (since not everybody knows what their credit score is, and it might be scary for some to find out 😅).

Easter egg

We also added an easter egg when tapping on the dolphin (you can check it out in the prototype, just tap on it like 4 times), which involved developers working with animation and haptic effects. I think everybody loved this part the most.

Simplify

But of course it's not just fun stuff. We also wanted people to understand their score and what they can do to improve it. So we worked a lot on the content to put this in the simplest way.

Design

The final result turned out to be just as we wanted it to be. It wasn't perfect from the beginning, but it was doing exactly what we wanted it to do. And it was a super nice feeling.

Outcome 🐬

We designed, developed and shipped our playful Credit score product in just a few weeks. It backed Anyfin's vision and drew users' attention. It also reminded us of the importance of delight in the user experience, and that we should always have room for that.

And we had a lot of fun doing it all.

Re-thinking refinance product in Anyfin app

Improving the core financial product

This product has been in place from the very beginning of Anyfin, so it had a lot of changes through all these years. And, even though each of them made sense, together they resulted in a bit scattered experience. So our focus was to make it nice and simple again.

Some background

The core product at Anyfin is loan refinancing. We help our customers lower their current interest rate, remove hidden costs, and generally make them feel in control of their loan situation.

And it is very important for active customers to know everything they should about their refinanced loans: from payments to extra benefits and features they can use to make it even simpler to deal with.

Before redesign

From the first glance there were no major issues. All features were in place, crucial functions were sort of prioritised. In other words, why should we even bother?

But, if we take a closer look...

In other words, the product was complex (no clear focus or hierarchy), not flexible (every new feature or important notification ended up in a stack on top of everything), and not adjusted to the new brand guidelines.

Redesign

Let's take a closer look at the new version.

We removed confusing navigation to put debt in focus.

We also added an option to refinance another in connection with the total debt

We connected all extra features to the total debt, since they affect it directly.

It will also serve as a “home” for every new secondary feature in the future.

Everything one can do about their upcoming payment is now in one place.

And this section takes the full user’s attention.

Change a payment plan or turn on automatic payments are now always visible and more accessible

Pay button is now the most prominent in the payment section

Important notifications and alerts are now contextually connected to the related items

Upsells and marketing messages calmed down and don’t steal user’s attention

Outcome 🏁

  • In a very short time we improved our core product for active customers
  • We made it ready for future improvements and experiments
  • We managed to keep all the functions, but made the overall experience smoother
  • Design improvements also led to old code reviews, which will make developers' work easier in the future

Endowment life insurance for Sberbank

The second stage

We were the first on the Russian market to launch Endowment Life Insurance online, but it was just the beginning. We got the first customers, first reviews and (of course) some problems that need to be solved in order to make the product better.

What did we learn

  • Not all customers fully understand how the product works
  • The product is lacking an interest rate. Not everyone is interested in insurance, therefore this benefit is not enough to attract customers
  • The coverage of the insurance is not good enough
  • Customer service is too hidden in the app
  • Customers do not see important information about the product and insurance terms, which results in negative reviews

What did the business offer

  • To make the product profitable for the client. This was not easy in terms of unit economics, but it was still possible.
  • To expand coverage, so that those who need insurance could consider our product more seriously

What did we offer

  • Conduct research and check the occurred problems in order to better understand their cause
  • Redesign problem areas to improve user experience
  • Integrate new features into the product without damaging the UX

Research is essential

Already at the MVP stage, we came up with a plan for future improvements. So, right after the first release we began to work on the second iteration. The main changes were adding an interest rate and covered cases. Shortly, prototypes were done, and user testing began.

Our first focus was the potential problem of hidden customer support. We wanted to find out if customers really needed it, and, if so, where they expect to find it. We prepared 3 different options and ran a quick test with 100 users.

Endowment life insurance for Sberbank

How did we become the first to bring the product online

The COVID pandemic began in 2020. Naturally, launching all Sberbank's products online became the top priority. Our team's main focus was to become the first on the Russian market to bring Endowment Life Insurance online.

What is endowment life insurance?

It is an insurance product that's often dressed up as a “College savings plan”. In other words, it's a sort of combo of life insurance and savings program.

Key issues

  • The product is complicated. One needs financial knowledge to understand it
  • The product is strictly regulated
  • None of the required processes were adapted for a digital environment
  • No other company had done it yet
  • Competitors were clearly working on a similar product

UX research

I joined the team when the first prototype had already been made. So, the first thing I did was usability testing to find out if there were any UX issues.

8 in-depth interviews were conducted, 1.5–2 hours each. Half of the respondents had experience in investing and knew about endowment life insurance, the other half had never invested or heard about the product.

Most of the issues were related to the copy. Respondents did not fully understand the product and its most important features. Other issues were quite small and required minor design changes. The most important thing was that we realised we were going in the right direction. So we moved on to the second iteration.

Design 🤝 Development

We needed to launch the product asap, so we worked very closely with developers. They started working on the implementation right after the first UX research was completed, and by the time we updated designs, the base for the future product had already been done. So the only thing left was minor UI and copy changes. This helped us speed up the process immensely.

Another thing we managed to accomplish during this project was a global design system upgrade. Using only the components we had at that point wasn't enough to deliver the best user experience, so we agreed to make some global changes that could be used by other teams in the future.

Language!

UX research showed us that the copy wasn't perfect in the beginning. Users misread some crucial terms and couldn't fully understand the product. So, together with UX writers, we iterated on every line a bunch of times. The key focus was to sound like a human and make all crucial details transparent and understandable.

Finally, the texts were written, and designs successfully passed the second usability test. The time had come for final approvals. Which, amongst other things, means the Legal team and their sign-off.

Since this product had never been launched online before, they wanted to minimise every possible risk. So they literally commented on every word we had written.

Blocking a friendly tone of voice and adding “ugly text” everywhere is quite common for big banks. And it is completely understandable, but in the end it makes it hard for people to use the product. So, naturally, we wanted to meet each other halfway and keep the end result as nice as possible. And, 4 iterations later, we made it.

Final result

Outcome 💫

We were the first on the Russian market to bring the full process of purchasing endowment life insurance online, and it only took us 4 months.

Next step – evaluating results, identifying occurring problems and moving on to the second iteration.

21 Pub

Concept of a service that serves as the bridge between online and offline for HoReCa

We all have been in this situation

It's Friday night, you and a bunch of friends want to go out
Question: where should you go?
You open up Google / Google Maps / TripAdvisor
Scroll through dozens of options, pick one
The one you picked is not what you wanted

Choosing a place is a lottery. Finding a new place to go takes a lot of time. Existing tools do not give enough information to make the right decision. Making a wrong decision is easy (bad audience, bad time etc). All these problems became the basis for the idea of creating a smart recommendation system which will help people make the right choice.

How it started

My wife and I were on vacation in London and certainly couldn't miss the traditional British pubs. The problem was there were way too many of them, so it was very hard to choose one. Ratings and maps didn't help much – they can never guarantee the “perfect match” and it takes a lot of time to go over them. Luckily enough, we had a bunch of friends there, so we followed their recommendations. But I wish we had a simple way of finding something that would meet our expectations.

After our trip I kept thinking about it and got an idea. A concept of a service which offers you a comfortable route between several pubs for the evening.

The concept had its advantages:

  • It solves the problem of finding a place for the evening
  • New places every time one's using the service
  • Gamified experience
  • Room for monetisation – native ads, special routes, challenges, etc.

And some problems:

  • The audience is limited because the main focus was on pubs
  • It didn't fully solve “Finding a place for the evening” problem. It didn't give user different options, so the whole thing was fully related on trust.

Research

I conducted 6 interviews to see if the main idea is interesting for potential users.

The most important things I learned:

  • Respondents found the concept fun and interesting, but were not sure if they would use the service often
  • Most respondents face the problem of finding new bars and restaurants

Digging deeper

After studying the case of searching for a bar, restaurant or any other place online, I realised that results often lack a personal approach, which makes it hard to make a choice. All existing solutions simply show a list of the nearest places, which is not very easy to use. Plus there is no guarantee that you will like the chosen place.

I assumed that this problem could be solved by making it easier to interact with the search results and recommending places based on user preferences.

I decided to dig deeper into this idea and conducted in-depth interviews with 15 respondents from 3 different groups:

  1. Students who are single or in a relationship
  2. 25 to 34 years old, working, in a relationship or with a family, with average or above average income
  3. 34–45 years old with family and above average income

UX research for a sales department

For the largest classified advertisements website

A client, one of the largest online marketplaces on the market, requested a big UX research for its sales department. Their main goals were to find bottlenecks and pain points in sales managers' daily work, and to come up with design solutions that could increase the productivity of the sales team.

As in every other company, the main tool for sales people was a CRM. So we agreed to focus mostly on digging deeper into it.

How it began

We planned 40 in-depth interviews with managers of different grades to assess the overall situation and identify as many potential problems as possible.

Each interview consisted of two parts:

  • Talking – researcher and designer asked sales managers about their daily work
  • Observation – we watched what and how did employee do during their working hours, what life hacks did they use in their work, how did they act on assigned tasks

We felt it was a good approach for 2 reasons:

  • The talking part helped us to understand how does the employee evaluate their work, what worries them, what processes seem to be difficult
  • The observation part allowed us to see the real work process. How many tasks do can they complete using their main tool, in what cases does it lack some functions, and what do they spend the most of their time on

To speed up the research results and prepare potential solutions quicker, we divided the respondents into 4 groups, 10 people each.

The first results showed:

  • CRM was overloaded and not intuitive enough. It could take an hour to solve one simple question
  • Many respondents were using offline tools - stickers, notes, etc., and then adding all the info to the CRM. This slowed down their work, and also made it difficult to share info with colleagues
  • There were many small logical errors in the CRM, which in the end resulted in a lot of wasted time and energy

First results presentation. Conflict.

After the first results were presented, a conflict broke out. In short, our client wasn't happy with:

  • Our speed
  • The format of the result. We chose to go with “problem cards”, which contained the context of the problem, it's criticality, where and when does it occur, and potential solutions. Unfortunately, we were expected to give more information.

Our client said that they discussed these points with our manager several times, and our manager said they didn't. Me and my co-workers didn't see any warning signs before the meeting, because we initially agreed that the manager would be the only contact point with the client from our side.

The situation didn't look good, no one wanted to step back. So I tried to help and suggested:

  • To involve the team in the communication with the client. It felt like this problem could be easily avoided if we had clear expectations from the beginning.
  • Adjust the research plan. Speed up the process and deliver results every week to meet the deadline.
  • Agree on what the final result should be and stick to it.

All sides agreed, and we managed to resolve the situation.

The second breath

After quite long negotiations, we needed to speed things up. To do it properly and to make sure we're all on the same page, we agreed on a new plan.

We also agreed on a format for the final results:

  • Keep the “Problem cards”. This format suited both business and development.
  • Create a Customer Journey Map to be able to dive deeper into the context of each problem and make it easier to share results across the company.

In three weeks we completed the survey on the remaining 30 respondents.

Outcome ✨

  • During the research, we identified more than 150 problems: UX-, business process- and employee training-related
  • 80% of the UX problems were already solved before the project finished due to early hand-off to the dev team. Remaining 20% were put in the next quarter backlog.
  • Employee training has been adjusted, all changes were based on our findings and recommendations
  • An action plan for business process problems has been developed together with key stakeholders
